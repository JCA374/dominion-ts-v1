A stronger teacher for the Steam advisor, inside three seconds

The advisor that sits beside your Steam Dominion games asks a teacher about every buy and every action play: each option is played out to the end of the game many times by the RL-v3 c2 net, and the option that wins most often is suggested. This project spends four days making that teacher stronger within the same 3-second budget, measured in paired matches against frozen c2 on fresh kingdoms, with the hope of beating Steam's Hard bots.

What this is

Dominion is a card game: each turn you play cards from your hand and buy one new card for your deck. The best model of this project family is not a network on its own but a search on top of one: at a decision the teacher (from bv-v1) copies the game, deals the cards it cannot see at random, and lets the c2 net play both players to the end, once for every option, on the same deal. Repeated 128 times, the option with the most wins is the advice. It beat plain c2 by 59-62% in earlier matches.

Example. Turn 6, you have $5: Laboratory, Market, Silver or nothing. Each of the four options is played out on the same 128 deals of your unseen cards and the opponent's hand. Laboratory wins 58 of 128, Market 55, Silver 51, nothing 40. The teacher only overrides the net's own pick when the gap is above 2 points and 2 standard errors, so it rarely gets fooled by luck.

The catch. In the advisor the teacher has 3 seconds per question on a laptop that also runs the game. A rollout to the end costs ~1.6 ms, so 3 seconds is ~1,800 rollouts shared by up to 11 options. Noise, not the net, is the limit: with 128 draws one option's win rate is known to about ±4 points.

Goal. A teacher that fits the same budget and beats today's advisor teacher, paired on the same fresh kingdoms (both seats), and beats plain c2 by more than today's does. Steam's Hard bot cannot be simulated here; frozen c2 (and GA and RL-v1 opponents for robustness) stand in for it.

Approach

No new training of the net (sg-v1 showed that PPO from c2 is flat). Instead, four levers on the search itself, each tested as a match arm (300 kingdoms × 2 seats against frozen c2), queued one after another:

  1. Value-net leaves. Stop each rollout D turns after the decision and score it with c2's own win estimate instead of playing to the end. D=4 is 5× cheaper and less noisy per draw (a number in 0..1, not win/loss), at the price of the value net's bias.
  2. Successive halving. The same rollouts, but spent on the leaders: after each round the worse half of the options is dropped (never your own pick), so the final comparison gets most of the draws.
  3. More cores. The advisor's teacher used one core. Several processes grading the same question and pooling their draws give 3-4× the draws in the same 3 seconds.
  4. The gate. With less noise the override thresholds (2 points, 2 standard errors) can be retuned.

        │                                   │
        ▼                                   ▼
 deal unseen cards (public view) ──► c2 plays both sides ──► end of game  or  value net after D turns
        │                                   │
        ▼                                   ▼
 paired win rates per option ──► override only if gain > margin and > z·SE ──► advice on screen
        │
 test: arm vs frozen c2, 600 games ──► Results tab ──► best arm into autopilot/teacher.py

Numbers: c2 is a 413-input, 2×256 MLP (181k weights); one rollout to the end ~1.6 ms on one core; a teacher game (buys + actions graded) takes minutes, so an arm of 600 games takes many hours on 7 workers.
